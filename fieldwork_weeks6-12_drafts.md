# WEEK 6 — *The proposal as a living blueprint*

**Pass —** *Turn:* a proposal is not a promise you're locked into; it's an instrument precise enough that you can *see* when the work has changed your mind, and honest enough to let it. · *Image:* a blueprint drawn in a medium you're allowed to redraw (closes Phase 1: the vast has become something holdable *and* revisable). · *Braid lead:* Emergent Learning (accessibility = a plan legible to others; DJ = success defined by the most excluded). · *Door:* the sincere object — the rigorous-looking proposal so fixed it can't survive contact with what it finds.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**A Plan That's Allowed to Learn**
*A proposal should be clear enough to guide you and loose enough to be corrected by what you find.*
Fieldwork · Week 6 · Part 1 · Emergent Learning · Accessibility · Disability Justice

There's a kind of research proposal that is admired and then quietly disobeyed. It's thorough, timelined, certain — and three weeks in, the work says something the plan didn't allow for, so the researcher either ignores the finding to protect the plan, or abandons the plan and works without one. Both are losses.

### Why
For five weeks you've narrowed a vast, honest care into something a real person could carry. The proposal is where it all becomes visible at once — objectives, method, significance, and how you'll know if it worked. The temptation is to make it impressive: fixed, final, defended. But a plan that cannot change is not rigorous; it's brittle, and brittle plans break exactly where the interesting findings are. *A proposal's job is not to predict the research but to make its changes legible* — to be the baseline against which you can see that the work has taught you something. Clear enough to guide, loose enough to learn: a **living blueprint**, with **success criteria** defined not by what would satisfy you but by what would change for the person your question named.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — participatory / adaptive proposal process. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** a project whose proposal was written *with* the affected community rather than for a funder alone. **What they did:** built the plan to be revisited — stated intentions and success criteria openly, and treated the proposal as a document that could be renegotiated as the work surfaced what mattered. **What changed:** the plan bent instead of breaking when findings diverged from expectation, and stayed accountable because the changes were visible, not hidden. **What it reveals:** rigour is not fixity; *a plan is trustworthy when a reader can tell what it promised and see honestly where reality revised it.*

**Carry into next week:** if your proposal is allowed to change, what is the one commitment in it that must *not* — the line whose breaking would mean you'd lost the person you set out to serve?

*Next week — Part 2: write the one-page blueprint, then hand it over.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**One Page You Can Redraw**
*A proposal should be clear enough to guide you and loose enough to be corrected by what you find.*
Fieldwork · Week 6 · Part 2 · Emergent Learning · Accessibility · Disability Justice

A blueprint fits on one page not because the work is small, but because anything longer stops being a guide and becomes a wall.

**Try — the blueprint on a single page.** Draft four things and nothing more: your objective (the question, scoped to what you can carry), your method (how you'll gather and make sense of evidence), its significance (what changes, for whom), and your success criteria (how any reader could judge whether it worked). *If it doesn't fit on a page, it isn't yet clear enough to guide you.*

**The activity — write the success criteria for the right person.** Define success by what would be different for the most excluded learner in your question — not by what would satisfy you or a supervisor. Then mark the one non-negotiable commitment, and the parts you expect the work to revise.

**Share — hand it over, ask what it would change.** Give the page to peers and ask the significance question directly: *what does this change for the people it names?* If they can't answer from the page, the significance isn't on it yet.

**Reflect — what you're willing to be wrong about.** A living blueprint asks you to hold intent and humility at once. Note where you most expect to be corrected; that's usually where the real inquiry is hiding.

*Artifact: a one-page research proposal (living blueprint) with explicit success criteria.*

# WEEK 7 — *Legal frameworks (the floor)*

**Pass —** *Turn:* the law is a floor, not a ceiling — it can hold you up, it can never tell you you've arrived; compliance is the height below which you may not fall, not the height worth reaching. · *Image:* the floor (Phase 2 opens). · *Braid lead:* Accessibility (DJ = who the floor still leaves outside; EL = adapting past the minimum). · *Door:* the sincere object — the fully compliant course that still failed someone.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Floor Is Not the Destination**
*Meet the law for what it is — a floor that holds you up, never a ceiling that says you've arrived.*
Fieldwork · Week 7 · Part 1 · Accessibility · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning

A course can satisfy every legal requirement for accessibility and still leave a learner sitting outside it. The paperwork is clean. The accommodations are on file. And someone still cannot actually take part — not because a rule was broken, but because every rule was followed and none of them reached far enough.

### Why
This week you meet the legal baseline — the **Accessible Canada Act**, the **AODA**, the **ADA** — and it matters that you know it cold, because it's the ground you stand on. But the danger isn't ignorance of the law; it's mistaking it for the goal. *A legal standard is the height below which you may not fall, not the height worth reaching.* Read as a ceiling, compliance becomes a stopping point: we're legal, we're done. Read as a floor, it becomes what it is — the place inclusion *starts*. The most useful thing you can do with legal literacy is turn it into a design question: of any standard you meet, ask **who does this still fail?** The law names a minimum; your work begins where the minimum runs out.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — ACA/AODA/ADA compliance in a real organisation. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** an organisation that had met its legal accessibility obligations. **What they did:** treated compliance as the starting line — auditing not just whether they met the standard, but whom the standard-as-met still excluded. **What changed:** they found gaps the law didn't name, because the law wasn't written to. **What it reveals:** legal compliance and genuine access are different measurements; *passing the legal test tells you that you haven't fallen through the floor, and nothing at all about how high you've reached.*

**Carry into next week:** you've met the floor — now, of the people your standard still fails, whose exclusion is technical (fixable by a better checklist) and whose is structural (fixable only by redesign)?

*Next week — Part 2: audit your own environment against the floor, and name what it leaves out.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Audit the Ground You Stand On**
*Meet the law for what it is — a floor that holds you up, never a ceiling that says you've arrived.*
Fieldwork · Week 7 · Part 2 · Accessibility · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning

The point of knowing where the floor is isn't to stand on it. It's to know exactly how far above it you have to climb.

**Try — locate your legal baseline.** Identify which framework governs your context (ACA, AODA, ADA, or a sector equivalent) and the specific obligations it places on your work. Get concrete: not "we're accessible," but *which* clause requires *what*.

**The activity — audit against the floor, then past it.** Two columns. Left: where you meet the legal standard. Right: where you meet it *and someone is still excluded.* The right column is the actual output — it's the map of everything the law can't see. *Use legal literacy as a design tool, not a finish line.*

**Share — compare the right columns.** With your group, pool the right-hand columns. Patterns across contexts are the ones worth building against; they're structural, not local.

**Reflect — the gap between legal and real.** Sit with the distance between "compliant" and "actually works for everyone." That distance is your design space for the rest of the year.

*Artifact: a legal-frameworks audit of your environment, naming what the law leaves out.*

# WEEK 8 — *Technical standards (the grammar)*

**Pass —** *Turn:* a page can pass every accessibility check and still reach no one — WCAG is grammar, not meaning; it makes a sentence legal, not worth reading. · *Image:* the grammar (Phase 2). · *Braid lead:* Accessibility (EL = content that teaches, not just parses; DJ = who "technically accessible" still alienates). · *Door:* the two rooms — built to pass vs built to work.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**Built to Pass, or Built to Reach**
*Technical standards are a necessary grammar for design — never its meaning.*
Fieldwork · Week 8 · Part 1 · Accessibility · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice

Two versions of the same lesson. One passes every automated accessibility scan — every image tagged, every contrast ratio correct, every heading nested right — and teaches no one, because it was built to satisfy the checker. The other has the same clean scan *and* a person on the other side who actually learns. From the outside they look identical. Only one of them reaches anybody.

### Why
This week you gain real command of **WCAG 2.1** — perceivable, operable, understandable, robust — and you should, because a page that fails these fails people concretely and immediately. But hold the harder truth alongside the skill: *a page can pass every check and still reach no one.* WCAG is grammar. Grammar makes a sentence well-formed; it cannot make it worth reading. A technically perfect page can still be pedagogically dead or culturally alienating — correct and useless at once. So the standard is a **necessary grammar**, not the meaning: you use it the way a writer uses spelling, as the floor beneath the work, never the work itself. The scan tells you the page is legible. It tells you nothing about whether it teaches.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a digital learning platform redesigned for WCAG. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** a platform meeting technical accessibility standards. **What they did:** looked past the passing scan to whether real learners could actually *use and learn from* the content — treating the standard as necessary and insufficient at once. **What changed:** they found content that passed every checkpoint and still failed people, and redesigned for use rather than for the checker. **What it reveals:** conformance and comprehension are separate achievements; *a checklist can certify that a door is technically openable while telling you nothing about whether anyone can find it.*

**Carry into next week:** when a page passes the scan but still fails a learner, who decides that it's failed — and is that person anywhere in your design process yet?

*Next week — Part 2: run a WCAG scan on your own content, then look for what the scan can't see.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Run the Scan, Then Read the Room**
*Technical standards are a necessary grammar for design — never its meaning.*
Fieldwork · Week 8 · Part 2 · Accessibility · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice

A scan is fast and honest about grammar and silent about meaning. So you run it — and then you do the part it can't.

**Try — scan one real piece of your own content.** Pick something you own and run it against WCAG 2.1 principles. Fix what the scan catches; those are real barriers. This is the grammar pass.

**The activity — find the page that passes and still fails.** Now hunt for the harder case: content that *passes* the checklist yet stays useless or alienating — dense, culturally narrow, technically legible and humanly closed. Name why it fails despite passing. *That gap between "passes" and "teaches" is where design actually happens.*

**Share — compare scans and the gaps beyond them.** With your group, compare not just scan results but the passes-yet-fails examples. The shared ones reveal where the standard itself is blind.

**Reflect — grammar you needed, meaning you still owe.** Notice how much relief a clean scan gives, and how little it guarantees. The standard is a beginning you can't skip and can't stop at.

*Artifact: a WCAG scan of one piece of your own digital content, plus a note on what passed and still failed.*

# WEEK 9 — *Disability justice (the compass)*

**Pass —** *Turn:* standards tell you where the floor is; only a compass tells you which way is right — and the compass was never in the designer's hand. It's held by whoever the design fails most. · *Image:* the compass (Phase 2). · *Braid lead:* Disability Justice (accessibility = the floor DJ moves past; EL = leadership becoming method). · *Door:* the single moment of friction — a decision made *about* people who weren't in the room.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**Who Holds the Compass**
*Move past single-issue compliance into intersectionality and the leadership of those most affected.*
Fieldwork · Week 9 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

A design decision gets made in a meeting. Everyone is thoughtful; everyone means well; the accessibility standards are open on the table. And the one group of people the decision most affects is not in the room — represented, maybe, spoken for, but not present. The room is doing its best, and it is pointed slightly wrong, because it's using a map with no compass.

### Why
The last two weeks gave you the floor and the grammar — real, necessary, and both silent on one question: *right for whom?* That's what **disability justice** supplies, and it's a compass, not another checklist. It insists on **intersectionality** — that disability compounds with race, gender, and class, so a single-axis fix leaves the multiply-excluded exactly where they were — and on the **leadership of those most impacted**: the principle that the person the design fails most is not a source of feedback to be gathered but a hand that should be on the wheel. *A standard can be met by people who were never in the room; a direction can only be set by the people the design is for.* The move this week is the hardest and most important in the series — from designing *for* to designing *with*, from expert to co-learner.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a disability-justice-led design process. Verify before publishing; Crenshaw on intersectionality (1989) is a real citation to confirm and place.]* **Context:** a process led by, not merely consulting, disabled people — with attention to who among them is usually left out. **What they did:** put decision-making power, not just input channels, in the hands of those most affected, and read the work through an intersectional lens. **What changed:** priorities shifted from what designers assumed mattered to what the most-excluded named as mattering. **What it reveals:** consultation and leadership are different transfers of power; *feedback lets people mark the map you drew, while leadership lets them choose the direction.*

**Carry into next week:** in your own context, who currently holds the compass — and what would you have to give up for it to pass to the people your work most affects?

*Next week — Part 2: map power and voice in your context, and name who is missing.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Map the Power, Name the Missing**
*Move past single-issue compliance into intersectionality and the leadership of those most affected.*
Fieldwork · Week 9 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

You can't hand over a compass you haven't admitted you're holding. So first, map who holds what.

**Try — map power and voice.** For your context, name who leads, who benefits, and who is most impacted — three lists that, in an honest map, rarely match. Where "most impacted" and "leads" name different people, you've found the week's real work.

**The activity — read your question through an intersectional lens.** Take your research question and ask who, at the intersections (disability *and* race, *and* class, *and* language), your current framing still leaves out. Rewrite so it can hold compounding exclusion, not a single axis. *A question that names only one axis of exclusion quietly serves the least-excluded of the excluded.*

**Share — find who's missing from every map.** Pool your power maps with peers. The people absent from *everyone's* map are the ones the whole field is structured not to see.

**Reflect — from expert to co-learner.** Notice what it costs you to move a decision from "I'll design it well for them" to "they'll decide with me." That cost is the honest price of the compass.

*Artifact: a power-and-voice map of your context, plus an intersectional reading of your research question.*

# WEEK 10 — *Emergent learning (the method)*

**Pass —** *Turn:* the old model treats the learner as an empty account to deposit knowledge into; the person in front of you already knows the thing your design most needs to hear. · *Image:* the method (Phase 2). · *Braid lead:* Emergent Learning (DJ = whose knowledge counts; accessibility = conditions for participation). · *Door:* the invisible default made visible — "banking" as the unseen water everyone swims in. · *Real source:* Freire, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1970) — confirm edition.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Account and the Conversation**
*Trade the model that treats the learner as an account to fill for one where learning is alive, relational, and shared.*
Fieldwork · Week 10 · Part 1 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

Picture the default training session: an expert at the front with the knowledge, learners in rows waiting to receive it, a test at the end to check how much was successfully transferred. It feels neutral — just how teaching works. It isn't neutral. It's a specific theory of people, and it's quietly wrong about the ones in the room.

### Why
Paulo Freire called that default the **banking concept** of education: the learner treated as an empty account, the teacher making deposits, knowledge flowing one way. *[Confirm the framing against* Pedagogy of the Oppressed *before publishing.]* Its problem isn't only that it's dull; it's that it's built on a falsehood — that the learner arrives empty. **Emergent learning** starts from the opposite fact: the person in front of you already carries knowledge your design needs, and the most important things are not deposited but *surface* between people, in relationship, when the conditions are right. *You cannot deposit understanding into someone; you can only build the conditions in which it emerges.* This reframes the designer's job entirely — you stop scripting the outcome and start creating the conditions that let it arrive, which means you have to be willing to be surprised by what your learners already know.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — Freirean / emergent pedagogy in a workplace or adult-learning setting. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** a learning environment that had run on the banking model. **What they did:** designed for emergence — collaborative dialogue, learners' existing knowledge treated as material rather than deficit, outcomes allowed to surface rather than be pre-scripted. **What changed:** learning appeared in places the old model registered as empty, because the people were never actually empty. **What it reveals:** the banking model doesn't fail because deposits are inefficient; *it fails because it was never true that the learner had nothing already — the emptiness was in the theory, not the person.*

**Carry into next week:** where in your context is learning *already* emerging on its own — in a hallway, a group chat, a peer fixing another's problem — that your formal design doesn't see or count?

*Next week — Part 2: find where learning already emerges, and design one condition for more of it.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Find Where It's Already Happening**
*Trade the model that treats the learner as an account to fill for one where learning is alive, relational, and shared.*
Fieldwork · Week 10 · Part 2 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

Before you design a single new thing, go looking for the learning that's already happening without you. It's there. It just isn't on the plan.

**Try — observe emergence in the wild.** Watch your context for where learning arises relationally and unplanned: peers teaching peers, questions answered sideways, understanding built in the margins of the official design. Write down what you see, without fixing it.

**The activity — design one condition, not one outcome.** Choose a single condition that invites more of that emergence — a structured dialogue, a peer-teaching slot, a genuinely open question with time to sit — and design it so it *facilitates* rather than *controls* what comes out. *Design the conditions; don't script the conclusion.*

**Share — critique the banking defaults.** With peers, name where each of your environments still treats learners as empty accounts. The defaults are so normal they're hard to see alone; a group makes them visible.

**Reflect — what you had to stop controlling.** Notice the discomfort of designing something whose output you can't predict. That discomfort is the method working.

*Artifact: a "where learning is emerging" observation, plus one designed condition that facilitates emergence.*

# WEEK 11 — *Neuroeducation & brain health*

**Pass —** *Turn:* designing for how brains actually learn isn't a favour to a few — the "normal" brain your training assumes is a fiction everyone else was forced to retrofit themselves to. · *Image:* the method, extended — the tool tuned to the real hand. · *Braid lead:* Accessibility + Emergent Learning (DJ = who the neurotypical default excludes). · *Door:* the invisible default made visible. · *Real sources:* spacing effect (Ebbinghaus); active-learning research; UDL/CAST — confirm specifics.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Brain the Design Forgot**
*Design with how brains actually learn — and catch the "neurotypical default" that forces everyone else to adapt.*
Fieldwork · Week 11 · Part 1 · Accessibility · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice

Most training is built for a learner who doesn't exist: one who absorbs information the first time, in one long sitting, with no emotion in the room and full attention on demand. Real brains don't work like that — not "impaired" brains, *any* brains. The design was tuned to an average nobody actually is, and everyone has been quietly retrofitting themselves to it ever since.

### Why
This week brings what the science of learning actually shows: that memory is built by **spacing** and retrieval, not single exposure; that **active engagement** beats passive reception; that emotion isn't noise in learning but part of its machinery. *[Confirm each claim against a named source before publishing.]* Designing with these isn't a special accommodation bolted on for some learners — it's building for how brains in general actually work. And here's the turn: the so-called **neurotypical default** most training assumes is itself a kind of retrofit demand, a narrow norm that forced the majority to adapt to *it*. *Designing for the range of real brains doesn't lower the bar for a few; it stops making everyone else compensate for a bar set wrong.* What looks like accommodation is, more honestly, correction.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a session redesigned with active learning and spaced repetition. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** instruction built on single-exposure, passive delivery. **What they did:** redesigned with spacing, retrieval, and active engagement, and audited the design for "neurotypical-by-default" assumptions. **What changed:** retention and participation improved across learners, not only those with identified needs. **What it reveals:** evidence-based design and inclusive design converge; *what we file under "accommodation" is often just design that finally matches how the brain was always going to learn.*

**Carry into next week:** which of your current design habits are "just how we train," and which of those are actually a demand that learners retrofit themselves to a norm no brain really fits?

*Next week — Part 2: redesign one session with the science, and audit it for neurotypical defaults.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Redesign for the Brain You Actually Have**
*Design with how brains actually learn — and catch the "neurotypical default" that forces everyone else to adapt.*
Fieldwork · Week 11 · Part 2 · Accessibility · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice

Take one session you already run and rebuild it around how memory and attention actually behave — then hunt the assumptions you didn't know you'd made.

**Try — apply the evidence to one real session.** Rework a single session or module with spaced repetition, retrieval practice, and active engagement replacing single-exposure delivery. Keep it concrete and small enough to actually run.

**The activity — audit for neurotypical defaults.** Go through the redesign asking, at each step, *which brain am I assuming?* Flag every place the design quietly requires sustained attention, one-shot memory, or emotional neutrality, and offer another route through. *An inclusive design gives more than one road to the same understanding.*

**Share — swap audits.** With peers, trade redesigns and look for each other's invisible defaults; the ones you can't see in your own work are usually obvious in someone else's.

**Reflect — accommodation or correction?** Notice how many "accommodations" turned out to be improvements for everyone. That's the sign the original default, not the learner, was the problem.

*Artifact: a redesigned session (active learning + spaced repetition), audited for neurotypical defaults.*

# WEEK 12 — *Identifying research gaps*

**Pass —** *Turn:* the richest gap is rarely an empty space no one has studied — it's a relationship between things studied thoroughly and never connected; you find it not by reading more, but by noticing what never touches. · *Image:* closing Phase 2 — the map of tools laid side by side, and the space between them where none of them reach. · *Braid lead:* all three, converging (the gap *is* their missing integration). · *Door:* the two rooms — the well-lit fields and the dark seam between them.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Space Between What's Known**
*The most generative research gap is a connection no one has drawn, not a topic no one has studied.*
Fieldwork · Week 12 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

You go looking for your research gap expecting a blank space — some unstudied corner the literature forgot. And you keep not finding one, because the important things all seem thoroughly studied. That frustration is a clue you're looking in the wrong shape. The gap you want isn't a hole in the field. It's a seam *between* fields that are each well-lit and never wired together.

### Why
Phase 2 handed you four bodies of knowledge — the legal floor, the technical grammar, the justice compass, the emergent method — each deep, each studied by people who mostly don't read each other. The synthesis this week is to see that *the gap is not missing knowledge but missing connection*: accessibility that rarely meets disability justice, pedagogy that rarely meets either, standards that almost never meet lived method. **Identifying the research gap** is the act of naming precisely where these thoroughly-studied things have never been made to work together — and that is the most generative position in the whole curriculum, because *an unstudied topic gives you a question no one has answered, but an unconnected relationship gives you a question no one has thought to ask.* Your inquiry lives in that seam.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a documented synthesis gap, or a study that named an unaddressed intersection of pillars. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** a field where each relevant area was well-developed in isolation. **What they did:** articulated the specific place where integration was missing, rather than adding to one silo. **What changed:** the framing opened a line of inquiry the separate literatures couldn't have produced alone. **What it reveals:** the frontier of a mature field is usually internal; *the last unmapped territory isn't past the edges of what's known — it's in the seams between the things we know best.*

**Carry into next week:** now that you can name the seam your inquiry sits in, what kind of evidence would actually let you speak into it — numbers, lived accounts, or both — and who has to gather it?

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Name the Seam**
*The most generative research gap is a connection no one has drawn, not a topic no one has studied.*
Fieldwork · Week 12 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

Your gap statement is one sentence you'll defend for the rest of the year. This week you write it, and then you let peers try to break it.

**Try — map the four bodies, then the space between.** Lay out what's well-established in each pillar area relevant to your question. Then mark, physically, where two or more have never been connected in your context. *The mark is your gap; the silos are just its border.*

**The activity — write the gap statement.** One or two sentences naming the specific missing integration your work addresses — not "no one has studied X," but "X and Y are each well understood and have never been brought together for Z." Precision here is the whole game.

**Share — stress-test it.** Bring the statement to peers and invite the hardest question: *has this actually not been connected, or have you just not found where it was?* A gap that survives that question is worth a year.

**Reflect — the generative position.** Notice that sitting in a seam is less comfortable than owning a silo — you'll be legible to fewer people at first. That discomfort is the cost of the most original position available to you.

*Artifact: a gap statement, stress-tested with peers — the hinge from Phase 2 into investigation.*
