# WEEK 19 — *Meaning-making (data analysis)*

**Pass —** *Turn:* analysis is where lived experience is most easily killed — coding turns a person's words into a category, and the whole discipline is to label them in a way you can still hear the voice through; a theme has to remember the person. · *Image:* sorting what you'll give back, without losing whose it is. · *Braid lead:* Emergent Learning / Disability Justice (accessibility = analysis others can follow). · *Door:* the sincere object — the immaculate coding scheme with the human gone out of it.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Theme That Remembers the Person**
*Analyse for pattern without flattening the lived experience back out of the data.*
Fieldwork · Week 19 · Part 1 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

Coding is quietly violent if you let it be. A participant said something specific, in their words, out of their life — and analysis asks you to turn it into a category, a code, a count. Done carelessly, the label swallows the person: you end with a clean thematic map and no one left alive inside it. You fought all through Phase 3 to keep lived experience *in* the data. Analysis is where it most easily leaks back out.

### Why
This week you make meaning from what you gathered — **thematic** analysis for the qualitative, **statistical** where the numbers earn it — and the central discipline is to do it without flattening. A code is a compression, and compression always loses something; the skill is choosing to lose the redundancy and keep the person. *A theme is trustworthy only if you can still hear the individual voice through the category it belongs to.* This is why you code **with a peer** — not for reliability alone, but because a second reader catches where your labels have started erasing rather than organising. Meaning-making that produces breadth (the pattern across people) and depth (the person within the pattern) at once is the goal; either alone is a partial truth wearing the costume of a whole one.

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Code It Together, Keep Them Whole**
*Analyse for pattern without flattening the lived experience back out of the data.*
Fieldwork · Week 19 · Part 2 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

Analysis this week is a two-person act, on purpose. You need another reader to catch where your categories start erasing people.

**Try — code a sample with a peer.** Take a slice of your qualitative data and code it independently, then together. Where you labelled the same passage differently, don't just reconcile — ask what each of you heard, and what the label was about to lose.

**The activity — keep the thread from theme to voice.** For each theme, keep at least one traceable line back to the specific account it came from. *If a theme can no longer point to a person who said it, it has flattened — rebuild it until it can.* Where numbers support a theme, use them for scale, not as the theme's replacement.

**Share — read each other's themes for lost people.** With the wider group, check whether anyone's clean themes have gone hollow. A hollow theme is usually a person who got coded away.

**Reflect — breadth and depth, not one or the other.** Notice any pull toward tidiness. Tidiness in qualitative analysis is often the sound of nuance being deleted.

*Artifact: coded data / thematic analysis (done with a peer), with themes traceable back to voices.*

# WEEK 20 — *Cross-case analysis*

**Pass —** *Turn:* the failure you found feels like your organisation's particular fault — cross-case analysis reveals it was never personal; it's a structure wearing your context's face, and seeing that both relieves and enlarges the work. · *Image:* holding your case up beside others to see the shared shape. · *Braid lead:* Disability Justice (accessibility = patterns that name whom systems exclude by design; EL = structural learning). · *Door:* the two rooms — the local story and the structural pattern behind it.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**Not Your Failure — a Pattern**
*Tell a local problem apart from a structural one — the difference between blame and design.*
Fieldwork · Week 20 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

When you first found the gap in your own context, it probably felt like a local failing — your organisation's, maybe partly your own. Then you set your findings beside other people's, and the same shape appears in settings that share nothing except the structure they're built on. That moment does two things at once. It lifts a weight (this was never just your failure) and it raises the stakes (this is not a local bug but a pattern by design).

### Why
This week you compare across cases, and the point is to distinguish a **local problem** from a **structural pattern** — because they call for completely different responses. A local problem invites a local fix and, often, quiet blame: fix your process, train your staff, patch your course. A structural pattern invites redesign, because the exclusion is built into how these systems are made, and it will regenerate anywhere the structure repeats. *A problem you can see in one place might be personal; a problem you can see in every comparable place is architectural.* **Cross-case analysis** is how you tell which you're looking at — and finding the pattern is what lets your work speak beyond your own walls, to everyone standing in the same structure without a name for it yet.

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Hold It Up Beside the Others**
*Tell a local problem apart from a structural one — the difference between blame and design.*
Fieldwork · Week 20 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

Your single case can't tell you whether your finding is local or structural. Other cases can. This week you borrow their light.

**Try — set your findings beside others'.** Compare your patterns with peers' findings, or with documented cases in adjacent contexts. Look specifically for the shape that recurs where surface details differ.

**The activity — name the structural pattern.** Write the pattern that holds across cases in one clear statement, distinguishing it from the local details that vary. *If it recurs wherever the structure recurs, it's architectural — say so, because that's the finding that travels.*

**Share — separate the local from the systemic together.** With your group, sort each finding into "local to this setting" or "structural across settings." The disagreements are where the real analysis lives.

**Reflect — relief and responsibility.** Notice both feelings: that it wasn't only your failure, and that a structural pattern is a bigger thing to have found. Both are true, and both point toward design rather than blame.

# WEEK 21 — *Backward design for social change*

**Pass —** *Turn:* most design starts from what we can deliver and hopes it adds up to change; backward design starts from the change and refuses everything that doesn't serve it — and the end is defined by the most marginalised learner, not the average one. · *Image:* deciding what "given back" should look like before building it. · *Braid lead:* Disability Justice / Emergent Learning (accessibility = an end-state reachable by all). · *Door:* the two rooms — designing forward from what's easy vs backward from what matters. · *Real source:* Wiggins & McTighe, *Understanding by Design* — confirm.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**Start From the End That Matters**
*Design backward from the transformation you want — defined by the most marginalised learner, not the average one.*
Fieldwork · Week 21 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

Most design runs forward from what's easy to build. You start with the content you have, the format you know, the activities that are convenient, and you trust that it all adds up to something worthwhile at the end. Usually it adds up to something — just not, reliably, to the change you actually cared about. The people it fails are the ones who were never at the centre of the forward guess.

### Why
**Backward design** inverts the order: you begin with the end — the transformation, the resilience, the specific difference you want to exist in the world — and design every step to serve it, refusing whatever doesn't. *[Confirm the Understanding by Design framing before publishing.]* The version this week demands one more move: you define that end **from the most marginalised learner**, not the average one. Designed for the average, a system reaches the middle and sheds its edges; designed backward from its hardest-to-reach person, it tends to include everyone on the way. *Design forward from what's convenient and you get what's convenient; design backward from who's excluded and inclusion stops being an afterthought and becomes the spine.* This is where your whole inquiry turns from diagnosis into intervention — from naming the gap to building the thing that closes it.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a backward-designed intervention centred on the most excluded learner. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** a design problem usually approached forward from available content. **What they did:** started from a defined end-state for the most marginalised learner and aligned every choice to it. **What changed:** the design reached its edges rather than only its middle. **What it reveals:** the starting point decides the ending; *a design aimed at the average excludes its margins by default, while one aimed at its margins tends to carry the middle for free.*

**Carry into next week:** you have the end-state — but every design hides assumptions you can't see from the inside; who has to *touch* the first rough version for those assumptions to become visible?

*Next week — Part 2: write the end-goal statement, and design backward from the most excluded learner.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Design From Who's Furthest Out**
*Design backward from the transformation you want — defined by the most marginalised learner, not the average one.*
Fieldwork · Week 21 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

Before you build anything, write the ending you're building toward — and write it for the person your field reaches last.

**Try — write the end-goal statement.** State the transformation you want to exist when this is done: what's different, for whom, and how you'd know. Make it concrete enough to design against.

**The activity — define the end by the margin, then work backward.** Rewrite the end-goal so it's defined by the most marginalised learner in your context, and map backward from it to the choices that would get there. *If the end-state works for the person your system reaches last, it will tend to work for everyone ahead of them.*

**Share — check whose end it is.** Ask peers: *whose success does this end-goal actually describe — the average learner's, or the excluded one's?* If it's the average, redesign the end before you design the steps.

**Reflect — convenient vs consequential.** Notice how much easier forward design felt. That ease is exactly what quietly sheds the margins.

*Artifact: an end-goal / transformation statement, defined by the most marginalised learner, to design backward from.*

# WEEK 22 — *Prototype development*

**Pass —** *Turn:* accessibility retrofitted is always a compromise; built in from the first rough draft it's cheaper, better, and honest — and you find your invisible assumptions only by handing the ugly prototype to the person your design forgot. · *Image:* the first draft of what you'll give back. · *Braid lead:* Accessibility / Disability Justice (EL = a prototype built to be changed). · *Door:* the invisible default made visible — the assumption you couldn't see until someone hit it. · *Real frame:* UDL / CAST — confirm.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**Born Accessible, Not Retrofitted**
*Build a rough, testable prototype with access designed in from the first draft — and test it with someone usually excluded.*
Fieldwork · Week 22 · Part 1 · Accessibility · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning

There are two ways access enters a design. One builds the thing first and adds accessibility at the end — a ramp bolted onto finished stairs, a transcript added to a shot video, always more expensive and always a little worse. The other builds it in from the first rough sketch, so there's never a version that excluded anyone to retrofit later. This week you make a prototype the second way, on purpose, while it's still ugly enough to change.

### Why
A prototype's whole value is that it's rough — cheap to build, cheap to be wrong, made to be tested and broken rather than admired. This week you apply **Universal Design for Learning** from the first draft, not the final polish, so the design is **born accessible** rather than repaired into accessibility. *[Confirm the UDL framing before publishing.]* *Access built in from the first sketch is design; access added at the end is apology.* And then comes the move that makes prototyping honest: you test it with someone your design would typically exclude. Every design carries assumptions its maker cannot see from the inside — about attention, body, language, prior knowledge — and *the only reliable way to find your invisible defaults is to watch the person you forgot try to use the thing.* The rougher the prototype when they hit it, the cheaper the lesson.

*[Sourced placeholder — a born-accessible prototype tested with an excluded user. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** an intervention that could have been built first and retrofitted later. **What they did:** applied UDL from the first draft and tested the rough version with someone typically designed out. **What changed:** invisible assumptions surfaced early, while they were still cheap to fix. **What it reveals:** the timing of access is the cost of access; *build it in at the sketch and it's nearly free, add it at the end and it's expensive, worse, and never quite the same design.*

**Carry into next week:** the first test exposed assumptions you couldn't see — but a single round of feedback is a snapshot; how do you make the *original* people it's for part of the design, not just testers of it?

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Make It Rough, Test It Honest**
*Build a rough, testable prototype with access designed in from the first draft — and test it with someone usually excluded.*
Fieldwork · Week 22 · Part 2 · Accessibility · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning

The goal this week is not a polished artifact. It's a rough one, built accessible from the first line, put in front of the right person fast.

**Try — build the born-accessible prototype.** Make a rough version of your intervention with UDL applied from the start — multiple means of representation, engagement, and expression built in, not added. Keep it rough enough that changing it costs nothing.

**The activity — test with someone your design forgot.** Put the prototype in front of a person your default would typically exclude, and *watch* rather than explain. Record where they struggle; each struggle is an invisible assumption becoming visible. *Design assumptions stay invisible until the excluded user hits them — so hit them early, on purpose.*

**Share — swap prototypes and blind spots.** With peers, test each other's rough versions; you'll spot defaults in their work that they can't see, and they in yours.

**Reflect — designed in or apologised for.** Notice how differently the design came together when access was there from the first sketch. That difference is the argument against retrofitting.

*Artifact: a rough, born-accessible prototype (UDL-applied), tested with a real, typically-excluded person.*

# WEEK 23 — *Iteration & public feedback*

**Pass —** *Turn:* a prototype isn't finished and then reviewed — the feedback *is* the design; and iterating with the original people it was for, not fresh reviewers, is what keeps it honest to whom it was always meant to serve. · *Image:* refining what you'll give back, with the people you'll give it to. · *Braid lead:* Disability Justice / Emergent Learning (accessibility = feedback everyone can give). · *Door:* the single moment of friction — the note you didn't want and needed. · *Real frame:* Schön, reflection-in-action / on-action — confirm.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Feedback Is the Design**
*Treat feedback as part of the making — and iterate with the people it was for, not a fresh audience.*
Fieldwork · Week 23 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

There's a comforting story where you build the thing, finish it, and then collect feedback as a final check before you're done. The story is backwards. In real design the feedback isn't a check at the end; it's the material the thing is made from. And *who* you go back to matters as much as that you go back — because it's tempting, once a prototype exists, to show it to a fresh, easier audience who'll admire it, rather than the original people who'll tell you the hard truth.

### Why
This week treats iteration as design, not inspection. You bring your prototype back to its **original stakeholders** — the people it was built for, the ones who saw it rough — and refine *with* them, honouring what they tell you rather than defending what you made. Donald Schön's distinction is the tool: **reflection-in-action** (adjusting live, as you watch it used) and **reflection-on-action** (rethinking afterward). *[Confirm the framing before publishing.]* *Feedback treated as the last checkpoint improves a finished thing; feedback treated as the design changes what the thing becomes.* Going back to the original people, specifically, is the discipline that keeps iteration honest — a fresh audience gives you applause, but the people it was for give you the truth, and only one of those makes the design better for them.

Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** a prototype that could have been finalised on the maker's judgement. **What they did:** ran structured feedback with the original stakeholders and refined in response, documenting what changed and why. **What changed:** the design moved toward what its people actually needed, not what its maker assumed. **What it reveals:** iteration has a direction of accountability; *a fresh audience tells you your work is good, and the people it's for tell you how it's failing them — and only the second kind of feedback is design.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Go Back to the People It's For**
*Treat feedback as part of the making — and iterate with the people it was for, not a fresh audience.*
Fieldwork · Week 23 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

Resist the pull toward a new, admiring audience. This week you return to the original people and let them change the design.

**Try — run a structured feedback session with original stakeholders.** Bring the prototype back to the people it was built for. Ask not "do you like it?" but "where does it still fail you?" — and make the session accessible in format and pace.

**The activity — document what changed and why.** Record the feedback, the refinements you made in response, and — honestly — the feedback you chose not to act on and why. *Iterating with the original people, not a fresh audience, is what keeps the design accountable to whom it was for.*

**Share — reflect in and on the action.** With peers, separate the changes you made live, mid-session (reflection-in-action) from the ones you saw only afterward (reflection-on-action). Both are the method working.

**Reflect — honoured or defended.** Notice whether your instinct was to defend the prototype or to let it be changed. The willingness to be changed is the whole point of a prototype.

# WEEK 24 — *Culmination & dissemination*

**Pass —** *Turn:* the project isn't finished when it's done — it's finished when it's given away in a form that can keep changing without you; a resource you still control was retained, not returned, and letting go is the last move from consumer to practitioner-researcher. · *Image:* giving it back — the whole phase image resolves; the door you can't stand in, held open for someone else. · *Braid lead:* all three, converging (accessibility so it can be received, DJ so it returns to whom it came from, EL so it stays alive). · *Door:* giving it back — and a quiet echo of the series' first image: the course was finished, someone was still outside; now the door is one they can keep opening.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**Finished Means Given Away**
*Return the work to the community as an accessible, living resource — completed by being let go.*
Fieldwork · Week 24 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

At the start of all this, a course was finished and someone was still outside. Twenty-four weeks later you have a tested, born-accessible intervention and a body of work that names a real pattern and does something about it. It would be easy to call that finished — to publish it, own it, and move on. But there's one more move, and it's the one that completes the whole journey: you give it back, in a form that can keep changing without you. *A resource you still control was retained; only a resource that can outgrow you was truly returned.*

### Why
**Dissemination** here isn't publication in the ordinary sense — releasing a fixed artifact under your name. It's **returning knowledge to the community** it came from, in a form that's accessible enough to actually reach them and alive enough to keep adapting after you let go. That last quality is the hard one. A living resource keeps evolving beyond its maker's control — which means relinquishing the authority to be its final word. *The move from consumer to practitioner-researcher completes not when you produce the knowledge, but when you release it to the people who can keep it alive.* This is where the three pillars finally resolve into one act: accessible so it can be received, returned to those it was drawn from, and emergent so it grows past you. The door you spent a year building is one you were always meant to hold open and then step back from.

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Hand It Over for Good**
*Return the work to the community as an accessible, living resource — completed by being let go.*
Fieldwork · Week 24 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

The last task of the year is also the hardest to do generously: finish the work by giving it away in a form that can live without you.

**Try — finalise the living resource.** Assemble your body of work into a usable resource for the community it serves — accessible in format from the ground up, and structured so others can adapt it, not just consume it.

**The activity — design for life after you.** Build in the means for it to keep changing: how others contribute, how it updates, how it survives your attention moving on. *A resource that can't change without you wasn't returned — it was just published.* Make the accessibility native (linear, keyboard- and screen-reader-friendly, alt-texted, transcribed), because a living resource that excludes people isn't alive to them at all.

**Share — return it to whom it came from.** Give it back to the original community and stakeholders, not just a public feed. Let the people it was for be the ones who receive it first and shape where it goes next.

**Reflect — the whole arc, and the letting go.** Look back across twenty-four weeks: from a gap you could only endure, to a question, to evidence, to an intervention, to a resource in other people's hands. Notice what it costs to release control of the thing you made — and that the cost is the proof you've become a practitioner-researcher rather than a keeper of answers. The course is finished. This time, no one is still outside — and the door stays open without you holding it.
