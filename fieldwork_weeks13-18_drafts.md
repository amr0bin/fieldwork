# WEEK 13 — *Choosing methodology*

**Pass —** *Turn:* a method isn't chosen to look rigorous; it's chosen to be *answerable* to the specific person your question named — rigour is accountability to them, not a credential you display. · *Image:* choosing how near to stand. · *Braid lead:* Emergent Learning / Disability Justice (accessibility = evidence everyone can be represented in). · *Door:* the two rooms — the method that impresses vs the method that answers.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**A Method That Has to Answer to Someone**
*Choose a methodology by what your question — and the person it names — actually require, not by what looks rigorous.*
Fieldwork · Week 13 · Part 1 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

There are two ways to pick a research method. One asks: what would look most rigorous, most defensible, most like real research? The other asks: what would actually let the person my question named be heard, at a scale that could change something? They often point to different methods — and only the second one is accountable to anybody.

### Why
You have a gap and a question with a face on it. Now you choose how to investigate — and the honest criterion isn't prestige, it's fit. A **mixed-methods** approach earns its place here for a specific reason: numbers give you *scale* (how widespread, how many, is this a pattern), and lived-experience inquiry gives you *depth* (what it actually is, for whom, why). *A method is rigorous not when it's impressive but when it's answerable to the people it claims to be about.* Choosing also forces the quiet, consequential question of **what counts as valid evidence** — because deciding that only numbers count, or only stories do, decides in advance whose reality gets to be real. That decision is methodological and ethical at once, and you make it on purpose or you make it by default.

*[Sourced placeholder — a mixed-methods inclusive study balancing scale and lived depth. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** an inquiry that could have defaulted to numbers alone or accounts alone. **What they did:** paired measurable breadth with lived-experience depth, and stated openly what each was for. **What changed:** patterns and meanings that neither method alone would have shown became visible together. **What it reveals:** method is a claim about whose knowledge counts; *choosing numbers or stories isn't a technical preference — it's a decision about whose reality is allowed to be evidence.*

**Carry into next week:** your method names how you'll gather evidence — but who is at the table when you decide *what to ask*, and is that different from who you'll gather it from?

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Defend What It's For**
*Choose a methodology by what your question — and the person it names — actually require, not by what looks rigorous.*
Fieldwork · Week 13 · Part 2 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

A methodology you can't defend in plain language is one you chose for how it looks. This week you choose one you can.

**Try — match method to question, not to prestige.** Write your question, then choose the methods that would actually answer it — the quantitative piece for scale, the qualitative piece for depth — and be honest where a "rigorous-looking" default would answer a different question than yours.

**The activity — write the defence.** In a page, state your methodology and, for each choice, *what it's accountable to*: what it lets you see, whose reality it admits as evidence, and what it would miss on its own. *State plainly what counts as valid evidence in your study, and why.*

**Share — pressure-test the fit.** Ask peers the useful cruelty: *does this method answer your question, or a nearby one that's easier to measure?*

**Reflect — impressive vs answerable.** Notice any pull toward the method that would look best. That pull is worth naming, because it's usually pulling you away from the person you're accountable to.

*Artifact: a methodology choice with a written defence of what it can and cannot show.*

# WEEK 14 — *Stakeholder engagement & co-design*

**Pass —** *Turn:* consultation asks people to approve a decision already made; co-design invites them before it's made — the whole difference is whether their "no" can still change the outcome. · *Image:* moving nearer — from the survey mailed out to the table shared. · *Braid lead:* Disability Justice (EL = design as shared act; accessibility = a table people can actually reach). · *Door:* the single moment of friction — the feedback session where it was already too late to matter.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**When the "No" Still Counts**
*Move from consultation-as-permission to genuine co-design — invite people before the decision, not after.*
Fieldwork · Week 14 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

A feedback session near the end of a project. The design is essentially finished; the room is being shown it and asked what they think. Someone raises a real objection — a good one — and everyone nods, and nothing changes, because there's no budget or time left to change it. The consultation happened. It just couldn't matter. Everyone in the room can feel the difference between being asked and being heard, even when no one says it.

### Why
This is the week to tell **consultation** and **co-design** apart, because organisations constantly perform the first while claiming the second. Consultation gathers input *about* a decision that's already been made; the people asked can approve or object, but the object has already hardened. Co-design brings people in *while the thing is still soft* — before the direction is set, when their contribution can still redirect it. *The test of real participation is simple: at this point in the process, can the participants' "no" still change the outcome?* If it can't, you're collecting permission, not co-designing. And the people most often consulted-too-late are exactly the ones the work most affects — which is how well-meant engagement quietly reproduces the exclusion it meant to fix.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a genuine co-design process with disabled participants. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** a design that could have been merely reviewed by those it affected. **What they did:** brought impacted people in before decisions were final, with real influence over direction, and mapped who was missing from existing feedback channels. **What changed:** the design shifted in ways late consultation could never have produced. **What it reveals:** timing is the ethics here; *the same feedback is participation before the decision and theatre after it.*

**Carry into next week:** you know *who* to bring to the table earlier — but how will you listen once they're there, so it's not just consultation moved slightly upstream?

*Next week — Part 2: map your stakeholders, find who's missing, and plan to invite them in early.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Map the Table, Find the Empty Chairs**
*Move from consultation-as-permission to genuine co-design — invite people before the decision, not after.*
Fieldwork · Week 14 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

Before you improve how you gather feedback, look at who your channels were built to hear — and who they were quietly built to miss.

**Try — map your stakeholders and your channels.** List who has a stake in your inquiry, then list how each currently gets to speak into it. Where a high-stakes group has no real channel, you've found an empty chair.

**The activity — plan the early invitation.** Design one concrete way to bring impacted people in *before* a decision is final — a co-design session, a shared-authorship step, a decision they get to make rather than react to. *Move at least one decision from "we'll show them" to "they'll help choose."*

**Share — check each other's missing chairs.** Trade stakeholder maps with peers; the group most invisible to you is often the one a peer spots immediately.

**Reflect — permission or participation?** Look honestly at your current practice and name where it's been consultation dressed as co-design. Naming it is the first move out of it.

*Artifact: a stakeholder map (with who's missing) and a co-design plan that invites people in early.*

# WEEK 15 — *Listening as a research practice*

**Pass —** *Turn:* extractive research listens to confirm what it came for; participatory listening listens to be changed — and the most important thing a participant says is usually the thing your questions didn't ask for. · *Image:* nearness at its closest — sitting with, not surveying. · *Braid lead:* Disability Justice / Emergent Learning (accessibility = interviews everyone can take part in). · *Door:* the single moment of friction — the answer that didn't fit the form.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**Listening for the Thing You Didn't Ask**
*Practise non-extractive, participatory listening — the kind that lets the answer change the question.*
Fieldwork · Week 15 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

In an interview, a participant starts to answer your careful question and then veers — into something you didn't ask about, that doesn't fit your categories, that your coding scheme has no box for. There are two instincts in that moment. One gently steers them back to the question. The other leans in, because *that*, the swerve, is very often where the real finding is. Which instinct you follow decides what kind of research this is.

### Why
Most research listening is a form of extraction: you arrive knowing what you want, you ask questions shaped to get it, and you treat everything else as noise. **Participatory listening** inverts the posture — you arrive willing to have your question changed by what you hear, and you treat the outlier, the thing that doesn't fit, as signal rather than static. *Structured questions can only return what you already knew to ask; the finding that matters most is usually the one your questions were built to miss.* This is also where research is most **non-extractive** or most extractive: whether people's accounts are taken from them to serve your pre-set frame, or received in a way that could reorganise it. With participants from marginalised communities, that difference is not subtle — it's the difference between being studied and being heard.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a participatory interview / focus-group practice with marginalised learners. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** an inquiry relying on lived accounts. **What they did:** listened for what structured questions would have excluded, and built the encounter to be non-extractive — participants shaping meaning, not just supplying data. **What changed:** the inquiry's own questions shifted in response to what was heard. **What it reveals:** listening is a research method with a direction of power; *you can gather someone's words to confirm your frame, or to let their frame correct yours, and only the second is participatory.*

**Carry into next week:** you've listened for depth and for the outlier — but how will you know whether what one person told you is a pattern or an exception, without flattening it into a number too soon?

*Next week — Part 2: run one participatory interview or focus group, and listen for what you didn't ask.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Sit Close, Ask Less**
*Practise non-extractive, participatory listening — the kind that lets the answer change the question.*
Fieldwork · Week 15 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning · Accessibility

You'll run one real listening encounter this week — and the discipline is to talk less and let the unexpected in.

**Try — hold one participatory interview or focus group.** Keep your questions few and open, built to surface lived barriers rather than confirm what you assumed. Make the encounter itself accessible — format, pace, and communication mode chosen with the participant.

**The activity — record what you didn't ask for.** In your notes, mark separately the answers to your questions and the things that arrived *unasked*. *Treat the unasked-for material as data, not digression — it's often the finding.*

**Share — bring the outliers to peers.** With your group, share the swerves, not just the tidy answers. Compare which outliers recur; a repeated outlier is a pattern your questions were structured not to see.

**Reflect — were you changed?** Ask yourself plainly whether anything you heard reorganised your question. If nothing did, you may have listened to confirm rather than to learn.

*Artifact: one participatory interview or focus group conducted, with notes on what was heard — including what you didn't ask.*

# WEEK 16 — *Quantitative data as provocation*

**Pass —** *Turn:* a number is not an answer but a sharper question — it tells you where to look, never what you'll find when you get there; and every survey quietly encodes whose "normal" it was written from. · *Image:* stepping back to see the pattern, then near again to read it. · *Braid lead:* Accessibility / Disability Justice (EL = numbers that open inquiry rather than close it). · *Door:* the invisible default made visible — the survey question that assumed a body.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Number That Points, Not Concludes**
*Use numbers as provocation, not verdict — and audit the survey for whose normal it was built on.*
Fieldwork · Week 16 · Part 1 · Accessibility · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning

A survey comes back with a clean, striking result — a percentage you could put on a slide and end the conversation with. The temptation is to treat it as the finding. But a good number rarely concludes anything; it points. It says *here, look here, something is happening in this direction* — and then hands the actual question back to you, larger than before.

### Why
Quantitative data earns its place in inclusive research, but only if you're clear about what it does. Its job is **provocation**: to surface patterns at a scale no interview can reach, to show you that something is widespread, to tell you where to bring your listening next. *A number shows you where to look; it never tells you what you'll find when you get there.* Treated as a verdict, the same number flattens the very people it was supposed to reveal. There's a second discipline this week too: a survey is not neutral. Every question encodes assumptions — about bodies, attention, language, "normal" — and a survey written from an **able-bodied or neurotypical default** will collect biased data cleanly and call it objective. Auditing the instrument for whose normal it assumes is part of the method, not a nicety.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a survey used as inquiry-opener, audited for bias. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** research that could have let a headline statistic stand as the answer. **What they did:** treated survey results as a prompt for deeper qualitative inquiry, and audited the instrument for able-bodied/neurotypical assumptions before trusting it. **What changed:** the numbers redirected attention instead of closing it, and the audit caught bias that clean data would have hidden. **What it reveals:** measurement is never neutral; *a survey doesn't just record reality — it decides, in its wording, whose reality it can record at all.*

**Carry into next week:** your numbers and your accounts are now both in hand — but as the study runs longer, how do you keep the consent and ethics you set at the start honest to what the research has actually become?

*Next week — Part 2: design and pilot a short survey, then audit it for whose normal it assumes.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Ask Three Questions, Then Interrogate Them**
*Use numbers as provocation, not verdict — and audit the survey for whose normal it was built on.*
Fieldwork · Week 16 · Part 2 · Accessibility · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning

A short survey done well beats a long one done carelessly. This week you build a small, sharp instrument — then turn its own logic on itself.

**Try — design three questions worth asking.** Write a three-question survey aimed at surfacing a pattern relevant to your inquiry. Keep it short enough that people finish it and pointed enough that the answers would actually redirect you.

**The activity — audit for the assumed body.** Before piloting, read each question asking *whose normal does this assume?* Rewrite anything that presumes a standard body, attention span, language, or mode. *A survey that assumes a normal collects bias and calls it data.* Then pilot it with a few people, including someone your default would have excluded.

**Share — compare provocations.** With peers, treat each other's results as questions, not conclusions: *what would you look at next because of this number?*

**Reflect — verdict or prompt?** Notice any urge to let a clean statistic settle the matter. That urge is the one to distrust.

*Artifact: a three-question survey, designed, audited for bias, and piloted.*

# WEEK 17 — *Ongoing ethical oversight*

**Pass —** *Turn:* ethics approval is a snapshot of a study that no longer exists — the research drifts as it learns, and consent given to the old study was never consent to the new one; oversight is the practice of staying honest while the work moves under you. · *Image:* staying near as the ground shifts. · *Braid lead:* Disability Justice (accessibility = ways to withdraw that actually work; EL = ethics that adapts with the inquiry). · *Door:* the sincere object — the approved protocol that quietly stopped matching the real study. *(Distinct from Week 5: there, consent as a living relationship at the outset; here, the drift of an in-flight study and the accountability that keeps it honest.)*

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**The Study Changed. Did the Consent?**
*Treat ethics as an ongoing relationship, renegotiated as the research itself drifts.*
Fieldwork · Week 17 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

You designed the study, got it approved, explained it to participants, and began. Then the research did what good research does: it taught you something, and you followed — a new question, a shifted focus, a different use for the data. Somewhere in that drift, the study people consented to and the study you're now running quietly became two different things. Nobody did anything wrong. The paperwork just stopped describing reality.

### Why
Week 5 built consent as a living relationship at the start. This week faces what happens *in flight* — because a real inquiry rarely ends up where its protocol said it would. **Ongoing ethical oversight** is the practice of noticing when the work has drifted far enough that the original consent no longer covers it, and going back. *Consent given to the study you described is not consent to the study you've become.* The same applies to **confidentiality** and **anonymity**: promises made under the first design have to be re-examined when the design changes, especially as findings sharpen and small communities become identifiable. Oversight isn't a committee you cleared once; it's an **accountability mechanism** you keep running — a standing habit of asking whether the people who agreed to this would still agree to what it is now.

### See
*[Sourced placeholder — a study that renegotiated consent/ethics mid-course as its direction shifted. Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** research whose focus evolved after ethics approval. **What they did:** re-examined consent, confidentiality, and anonymity against the changed study rather than the approved one, and built a mechanism to keep doing so. **What changed:** participants were re-consented to the study as it actually was, and identifiability risks that emerged later were caught. **What it reveals:** ethics is a moving relationship, not a fixed clearance; *the protocol you were approved for describes a study that stops existing the moment the research starts to learn.*

**Carry into next week:** you've kept your own study honest — but a finding you reached alone is fragile; who else needs to look at it before you trust that it's real?

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Re-Read the Promise You Made**
*Treat ethics as an ongoing relationship, renegotiated as the research itself drifts.*
Fieldwork · Week 17 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

Put your original consent and confidentiality plan next to your actual study, and look for the daylight between them.

**Try — audit consent against reality.** Line up what participants agreed to against what your research has become. Mark every place the study drifted past its original description; each mark is a promise that may need renewing.

**The activity — build the accountability mechanism.** Design a standing practice — not a one-off — for re-checking consent, confidentiality, and anonymity as the work continues: when you'll revisit, how someone withdraws now, how you'll catch rising identifiability in small communities. *Make oversight a habit with a schedule, not a memory you hope to keep.*

**Share — surface each other's drift.** With peers, describe how your studies have changed since approval; drift is easiest to see in someone else's work and easiest to excuse in your own.

**Reflect — would they still say yes?** Ask the plain question of your current study: if you re-explained it today, would the people who agreed at the start agree now? Design as if the answer matters, because it does.

*Artifact: a reviewed consent/confidentiality protocol and a working accountability mechanism.*

# WEEK 18 — *Collaborative inquiry*

**Pass —** *Turn:* a finding you reached alone is a hypothesis wearing a conclusion's clothes; a finding that survives other people trying to see it differently is the first thing you've got that's closer to knowledge than to opinion. · *Image:* nearness widened — the circle instead of the pair. · *Braid lead:* Emergent Learning (DJ = whose perspectives are in the room; accessibility = a session everyone can think inside). · *Door:* the two rooms — the finding that looked solid alone and dissolved in company.

## Part 1 — *Why, See*

**What Survives Other Eyes**
*Test your findings in company — the patterns that hold across perspectives are the ones worth trusting.*
Fieldwork · Week 18 · Part 1 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

Alone with your data, a pattern can look unmistakable. You've lived with it; it fits what you expected; it clicks. Then you lay it in front of people who don't share your assumptions, and one of two things happens. Either it holds — it survives their different angles — or it quietly comes apart, and you realise the pattern was partly your own reflection in the glass. Both outcomes are gifts. Only the first is a finding.

### Why
This week treats sensemaking as something you can't finish by yourself. **Collaborative inquiry** — bringing preliminary findings to peers for collective interpretation — isn't a courtesy or a validation ritual; it's how a private impression becomes a shared claim. *A finding you reached alone is a hypothesis; a finding that survives other people trying to see it differently is beginning to be knowledge.* Different perspectives don't just check your work; they cross-pollinate it, surfacing readings you were structurally unable to reach from inside your own frame. The most valuable thing a peer can do is fail to see what you saw — because a pattern that only appears from your vantage point was never really in the data. What survives the room is what you can build on.

Verify before publishing.]* **Context:** an inquiry whose early findings had been interpreted mostly by one researcher or team. **What they did:** hosted structured collective interpretation across perspectives, treating disagreement as data. **What changed:** some patterns strengthened and some dissolved, and new readings appeared that no single vantage produced. **What it reveals:** interpretation has a quorum; *a pattern visible only from where you stand is a feature of your standpoint, not yet a finding in the world.*

## Part 2 — *Try, Share, Reflect*

**Bring It to the Room**
*Test your findings in company — the patterns that hold across perspectives are the ones worth trusting.*
Fieldwork · Week 18 · Part 2 · Emergent Learning · Disability Justice · Accessibility

This week's work happens out loud, with others. You host the session where your findings meet perspectives that aren't yours.

**Try — host a preliminary-findings session.** Gather peers (and, where appropriate, participants) and present early patterns as provisional, not settled. Ask them to interpret, push, and disagree.

**The activity — sort what held from what dissolved.** Afterwards, separate the findings that survived multiple perspectives from those that only held from your vantage. *Keep what survived company; treat what dissolved as a lesson about your own standpoint, not a loss.* Note the readings that only emerged because others were in the room.

**Share — this week, hosting *is* the sharing.** The discipline is to present findings you're still willing to lose, and to let the group's disagreement do real work.

**Reflect — solitary certainty vs shared knowledge.** Notice how confident you were alone, and what changed in company. That gap is why inquiry needs more than one mind.
