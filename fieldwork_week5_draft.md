# PART 1

**What You Can Carry the Whole Way**

*Feasibility is the first ethical decision you make — and consent is a relationship, not a form.*

Fieldwork · Week 5 · Part 1 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

---

Somewhere in a folder there is a consent form with a real signature on it. The day it was signed, everyone in the room meant it. The study was explained plainly; the participant asked good questions; the box was ticked, the date written, the page filed. Nothing about that moment was careless. And that is the problem — a signature is a photograph of a single instant, and the study it belongs to will run for months. By week ten the person may feel differently, and the form in the folder will not know.

## Why

Last week you narrowed your question until it named someone. This week the room gets quieter and more practical, and it can feel like a demotion. Be realistic, people say. Count your hours. Look at your budget, which is probably nothing. Be honest about the access you actually have. It sounds like the week your ambition is measured and found too large.

It isn't. **Feasibility** is the first ethical decision you make, not a retreat from the ethical ones. A research question is a thing you pick up and carry — for twenty-four weeks, through your real job and your real life, alongside everyone who agreed to be carried with it. And the cruelest outcome for a participant is not being turned away at the start. It is being taken up — their time spent, their trust extended — and then set down in week fourteen, when the hours you never had run out. *A study you cannot complete is a promise broken to the people most used to being dropped.*

So scoping honestly is not the opposite of ambition; it is what keeps ambition from becoming a debt someone else pays. You are not cutting the dream down. You are deciding what you can carry the whole way without dropping anyone.

The same logic reaches consent. A form is a moment: permission transferred once and filed. But the people this inquiry is built for have most often met consent as exactly that — a document that asked once, took what it needed, and never came back. **Consent as a relationship** assumes the person may change their mind; that what was fine in week two may not be fine in week ten; that the right to withdraw has to stay warm and reachable the whole way through. Treated that way, ethics stops being a gate you pass at the start and becomes part of the design itself — a thing you build, not a thing you clear.

## See

**In one line:** a multi-year community-based participatory research project in which adults with developmental disabilities were not subjects of the study but part of the body that governed it.

**Context.** Community-based participatory research on health and safety for adults with developmental disabilities — a population routinely studied *about* and rarely *with*, and one for whom a one-time consent form has often been the whole of their say. *[Confirm setting, population, and framing from the primary source.]*

**What they did.** Rather than collecting consent once and proceeding, the project built standing community advisory structures that stayed involved across the life of the work — guiding decisions, not just approving them at the outset. Consent and direction were treated as things that had to be renewed as the research changed, which meant the design absorbed the cost of that relationship instead of pretending it was free. *[Confirm the specific governance structure and how ongoing involvement worked, from the source.]*

**What changed.** Keeping the relationship live surfaced what a single sign-off would have hidden — where the work needed to slow down, what participants would and would not carry, where an outside team's assumptions had to give way. It also made plain what such research actually costs in time and capacity, which is itself the feasibility lesson. *[State only what the source documents; soften or cut any outcome you cannot cite to a page.]*

**What it reveals.** The project makes visible what a consent form conceals: that permission is not a moment but a *relationship maintained over time*, and that a study's real scope is however much of that relationship you can honestly sustain to the end. Feasibility and ethics turn out to be the same question asked twice — *what can you carry all the way without dropping the people who trusted you to finish?*

## A question to carry into next week

If consent has to stay a living relationship for the length of your study, what is the honest maximum length your life can actually hold — and does your question still name the same person once you scope it to that?

# PART 2

**Weigh It Before You Carry It**

*Feasibility is the first ethical decision you make — and consent is a relationship, not a form.*

Fieldwork · Week 5 · Part 2 · Disability Justice · Accessibility · Emergent Learning

---

You know the weight of a bag only once it's on your back and you're a mile from the car. Research is the same. This week you weigh it first — before anyone has agreed to walk with you.

## Try — an honest accounting of what you can carry

Most feasibility failures aren't dramatic. They're a slow accumulation of hours you assumed you had. So start by counting what is real, not what is hoped: the hours per week you can actually protect, the money that exists (often none), the access you genuinely have to people and spaces, and the energy left after your actual job. *The point of the count is not to shrink your ambition but to find the version of it you can finish.*

## The activity — build the feasibility and resource map

On one page, lay out four columns: **time**, **resources**, **access**, and **consent**. Under each, write what you have, not what the ideal would require.

- **Time** — protected hours per week, across twenty-four weeks, with the weeks you already know will collapse (holidays, deadlines) marked honestly.
- **Resources** — money, tools, space, and any institutional support you can actually name, not assume.
- **Access** — who you can genuinely reach, and — the harder line — who you cannot, and what that excludes from your question.
- **Consent** — not whether you'll get it, but how you'll *keep* it: how someone changes their mind mid-study, how the right to withdraw stays reachable, who checks in and when.

Then draw the scope that all four columns can support at once. Where the map says your question is too large to carry the whole way, narrow it here — on paper, while it costs nothing — rather than in week fourteen, where it costs someone their trust.

## The consent plan — permission that stays alive

Write the plan as a relationship, not a document. Name the moment consent is first given, and then name every point afterward where it is *renewed*: a check-in when the direction shifts, a plain way to pause or withdraw, a record of what the person agreed to *this time*, not just at the start. **Ongoing informed consent** is simply this — treating the person as someone you remain in conversation with, especially when the research changes under you.

## Share — say the number out loud

Bring your one-page map to your peer group and read the scope aloud. Ask the two questions that are hard to ask yourself: *Is this honestly carryable for twenty-four weeks?* and *Once I've scoped it to what I can carry, does it still centre the person I named last week — or did the cuts quietly move them back to the margin?* Ask peers, too, what institutional or community support you actually have; we routinely under-count the help that exists and over-count the help we imagine.

## Reflect — what scoping down made you see

Scoping down is a small grief, and it's worth naming rather than skipping past — the study you can carry is smaller than the study you pictured. But notice what the honesty bought: a scope you can finish, a consent you can keep, and a promise you won't have to break. The map you made is not a limit on the work. It is the first evidence that you intend to be there at the end.

*Portfolio artifact this week: a one-page feasibility and resource map, plus an ongoing informed-consent plan.*
